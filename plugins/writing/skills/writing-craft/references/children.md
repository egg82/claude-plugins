# Children's text

Picture books, early readers, middle grade and educational text for children. Most evidence here is about picture
books; middle grade and educational text had almost none, so for them start from the adult references and the
inversions below.

## Which adult rules hold, bend or invert

| Adult rule | For children | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A moral said outright is a tell (SKILL.md, `llm-tells.md`) | **Holds, more strictly.** Never state the lesson anywhere in the story; show a character living it and changing | practitioner, Kole, below |
| Leave the last step to the reader | **Splits.** Leave the moral to the child; spell out what the child needs to follow the plot; leave to the pictures what the art will show | practitioner, Kole; Mem Fox, below |
| A tidy, upbeat ending is a tell | **Bends.** A resolved ending is the genre's norm, and a story without emotional resolution fails. The tell is the ending that states the lesson | practitioner, Kole; measured, unverified, Rezapour et al., below |
| Rhyme by default is a tell (`verse.md`) | **Bends.** Rhyme is a picture-book convention, but it must scan and must never flip word order for the rhyme | practitioner, Writers Helping Writers, below |
| The short everyday word (`editing-pass.md`) | **Inverts for read-alouds.** A read-aloud runs two to three grade levels above what the child reads alone; reach for words above their level | practitioner, library and Lexile guides, below |

Dialogue tags (`dialogue.md`'s "said, no adverb") and softened dark registers were not researched for children's
text; judge them against published books of the same age band.

## Never state the lesson

- **Kids are told what to do all day and don't want it from a book.** Show a character acting kindly and others
  responding; never "Gosh, kindness is so great". Put explanation for a non-fiction book in back matter (practitioner,
  Mary Kole, https://www.goodstorycompany.com/blog/picture-book-lessons).
- **Test: remove the lesson from the ending.** If the story still stands, it isn't didactic; if the plot existed only
  to deliver the moral, rebuild it from a character and a conflict (practitioner, Kole,
  https://kidlit.com/preaching-in-picture-books/). Her example of a failing ending: a girl closes the book with a line
  about letting her sisters pick the movie.
- **SCBWI's critique checklist** asks writers to beware of being didactic or preachy (policy, snippet,
  https://scbwi.org/).
- **Models build backwards from a moral** and end on the line that states it. They also default to care and kindness
  as the moral and leave arcs without emotional resolution (measured, unverified: search summary of Rezapour et al.,
  Findings of EMNLP 2025, https://preview.aclanthology.org/new-sigs/2025.findings-emnlp.1029.bib). Pick the story's
  value from the character's own problem.

## Picture books

From picture-book editors and writers (practitioner, https://writershelpingwriters.net/2026/01/picture-book-tips-and-tricks/,
and Mem Fox via https://www.instituteforwriters.com/4-questions-about-writing-picture-books):

- **Under 500 words,** non-fiction a little more. Art notes don't count and editors strip any that aren't needed.
- **The text doesn't say what the art will show.** Leave the illustrator room: no stage directions, and no scene
  without visual variety, such as two characters talking in a kitchen for three spreads.
- **The child solves the problem,** not an adult.
- **Read it aloud.** Short sentences with rhythm, filler like "that" and "just" cut.
- **End on a twist or an extra** that invites a second read.
- **A child listens to it.** Write for a child's ear and an adult's voice; a reading-level formula can't judge it. Lexile
  measures text difficulty only, and picture books routinely carry high Lexile numbers because an adult reads them
  (practitioner, snippet, library guides via https://retoldw.perfectionlearning.com/kindergarten-read-alouds.html).

## Vocabulary

Human-written stories tended to go above young readers' vocabulary level while AI stories held it safely at level,
and children and parents rated the two close (measured, unverified: search summary, Chinese fairy tales,
first and second graders, https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9709/12/4/139). If it holds, a model's children's prose reads
flat because it never stretches. Give the child a few words worth learning, made clear by the picture or the
sentence around them.

## Sameness across books

A collector of about 220 AI children's books on Amazon found them "all the same" (opinion, about illustrations
only, https://blog.coredump.cx/p/ai-childrens-books-body-horror-edition). Flagged AI children's books were caught by
cover errors, invented author profiles, copied series branding and extreme reviews: packaging, not prose (opinion,
secondhand, https://aiweekly.co/alerts/amazons-ai-generated-childrens-books-flood-gift-piles). No published list of
prose tells for children's text was found. Review a batch of drafts side by side for the repeated moral, the
repeated arc and "heartwarming" as the default register (one writer's impression, unverified).

## Judging

Children and parents rated AI stories close to human ones in the one study found, so a child's or parent's
approval says little about whether the text is good. Use editors, librarians or teachers who read many books of
that age band (`judging.md`), and read it aloud to a child of that age for whether it holds attention.

## Where the evidence is thin

The anti-didactic rule and the picture-book craft rules are consistent practitioner advice. Every measured claim
about LLM children's stories here came through search summaries. Middle grade, early readers and educational text
have no evidence of their own; Beck and McKeown's work on teaching vocabulary was not reached.
