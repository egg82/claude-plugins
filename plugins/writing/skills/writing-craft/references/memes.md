# Memes and internet-native humour

Captions, template memes, "Nobody: / Me:" posts and other formats a community fills in. General joke craft is in
`humour.md`. The evidence here is thin: two studies on model-made memes, the rest practitioner and opinion.

## How a format works

- **A format is a fixed frame with one open slot.** The frame is the agreement; the slot holds something specific
  the audience recognises. In "Nobody: / Me:" the joke is the gap between nobody asking and the oddly particular
  thing done (practitioner, https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/nobody). Know Your Meme's snowclone category defines
  the same split: what stays fixed, what may change (snippet,
  https://constructions.journals.hhu.de/article/download/647/707).
- **The slot is where models fail.** A generic behaviour anyone might do gives the reader nothing to recognise. Fill
  it with a particular object, act or number, and recognisability does the work (judgement, from the Know Your Meme
  examples).
- **A new item shows it knows its siblings.** A meme is a set of items sharing content, form and stance, copied and
  altered by many people; an isolated joke in the shape of a format ignores that (practitioner, Shifman, summaries
  only, https://tcjournal.org/?p=111). Stance (earnest or ironic) is part of the format, so match it.

## Rules

- **Formats age, and a dead one reads as corporate.** The failure readers name is the brand arriving after the
  trend, or using a template and slang as surface (opinion,
  https://hackernoon.com/the-problem-with-brands-chasing-memes). Prefer a format still alive in the audience's own
  venue over a fresh one posted late. A late post is worse than none.
- **Use a format only if you can say why it is funny.** A format can be funny because of who uses it, and a writer
  who cannot say why is copying the surface (opinion, same source).
- **Never explain or label the joke.** "We're being so relatable" and #relatable kill the subtext; keep the post
  short and a little detached (opinion, same source, secondhand through a search summary).
- **Keep one voice across trends.** Duolingo's and Ryanair's long-built personas absorbed trends because the trend
  fit the persona (opinion, same source).

## What models do

- **Reading an image memetically is weak.** Models read the visual too literally, miss the metaphor, copy text from
  inside the image and invent details. Humans led by 18 to 37 points on correctness, completeness and faithfulness;
  captions improved when the post title was given (measured, 30 memes, 2023 models, may not hold now,
  https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.13703). Give the model the situation the meme maps onto, in words.
- **AI-written memes average well and never top the list.** GPT-4o, given a 10-word image description and a topic,
  rated highest on mean funny, creative and shareable, but humans took most top-four humour slots, and human-AI
  teams produced more ideas, not better ones (measured, 150 memes per condition, crowd raters, one session,
  https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11433v1). Stock templates on a relatable topic such as work are where the average is
  strongest.
- **Generate many and let a human pick.** The ceiling is a person's choice, and the model supplies volume
  (judgement, from the study above).
- **Wordplay and cultural reference across modalities are the weakest part.** Pun memes and panel ordering sit below
  human level (measured, survey, snippet, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.19011).

## Gaps

- No source found on detecting that a format has gone stale. The only test is a person inside the community, who can
  say whether it still circulates.
- Nothing measured on irony layers; the sincere, ironic and post-ironic framing is journalism and student writing
  (opinion).
- Both model studies use 2023 to 2024 models and crowd raters, not the venue's own readers.
