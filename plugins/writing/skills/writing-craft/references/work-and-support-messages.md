# Work and support messages

Workplace email, customer-support replies, apologies, cover letters, LinkedIn posts and Slack. Relational email,
founder email, forum posts and general chat tells are in `conversational-and-web-copy.md`; read its tells section
first, since they all apply here.

**The common failure is a message that could go to anyone.** Hiring managers say they object to generic letters
more than to AI use, and support guides say the same of stock replies. Every rule below comes back to naming the
specific thing: the problem, the fix, the project, the question.

## Email at work

- **Length depends on the relationship.** In 5.3 million mostly warm Gmail messages, 50 to 125 words drew the most
  replies (measured, Boomerang 2016, https://www.nextgov.com/digital-government/2016/02/what-makes-people-actually-respond-your-emails/125925/).
  For cold email, 20 to 39 words did best in 34 million sends (measured, vendor, snippet,
  https://hunter.io/blog/cold-email-word-count).
- **Ask one to three questions.** More than three loses the gain (Boomerang, above).
- **Slightly warm or slightly negative beats neutral, and beats effusive.** Moderate flattery helped; heavy flattery
  hurt (Boomerang). A uniformly effusive register is on the losing side.
- **The ask goes where it is seen:** first line, or alone at the end (`editing-pass.md`, rhythm).

## Support replies

- **Name the problem and the action taken.** "Our apologies for any inconvenience this may cause" says neither.
  "I'm sorry the export failed and deleted your report. I've restored it, and it should be in your account now"
  says both (practitioner, Help Scout, https://www.helpscout.com/blog/customer-service-phrases/).
- **Say what you can do,** in place of "unfortunately I can't". Keep the customer in the channel they chose. Ask
  "anything else?" only once the problem is solved (Help Scout).
- **Write "I", one person doing one thing,** in a reply from one agent to one customer; a public page speaking for
  the organisation uses "we" (`plain-language.md`). In over a thousand real service emails from one retailer, plus
  experiments, agents writing "I" were read as acting for the customer and raised satisfaction and purchases;
  "we", the scripted default, did worse (measured, snippet, Packard, Moore and McFerran, *Journal of Marketing
  Research* 2018, via MIT Sloan Management Review). "I'll look into this", not "Our team will look into this".
- **A stock answer is easy to spot and makes the team look bad.** Keep macros for questions that truly recur; when
  the fix isn't ready, send a short personal acknowledgment instead of silence (practitioner, snippet, Intercom,
  https://www.intercom.com/help/support-and-retain-customers/our-best-practice-guide-to-customer-support).
- **Tells:** "Thank you for reaching out", "I understand your frustration", "We apologize for any inconvenience",
  each with no named problem and no action after it.
- **Here, a balanced, non-combative register is right.** The rule in `conversational-and-web-copy.md` to take a
  position is for posts and threads. In support, take a position on the facts ("that charge shouldn't have
  happened") and stay level in tone.

## Apologies

In scenario experiments with 755 people, the parts of a written apology ranked by effect were (measured,
Lewicki et al. 2016, via the university's release, https://news.osu.edu/the-6-elements-of-an-effective-apology-according-to-science/):

1. Acknowledge responsibility ("I was wrong").
2. Offer repair ("I'll fix what is wrong").
3. Tied: express regret; explain what went wrong; say it won't happen again.
4. Ask for forgiveness: the weakest, and the first to cut.

More of the parts rated better. A model apology tends to lead with regret ("I'm so sorry for any confusion"), skip
ownership and repair, which are the two strongest, and add softeners (judgement). "Sorry for the inconvenience" on its own is
the generic form every support guide warns against (opinion, vendor blogs, https://www.livechat.com/success/sorry-for-the-inconvenience;
its cited figures could not be traced, so don't quote them).

"I'm sorry you feel that way" is the non-apology. "You're right, that charge shouldn't have happened, and I'm
sorry. I've refunded it today" holds three of the parts in two sentences (Help Scout, above).

## Cover letters and applications

- **Hiring managers object to generic letters more than to AI.** Vendor surveys put rejection for AI use alone
  anywhere from about a fifth of managers to most of them, with different questions in each, while most say
  uncustomised letters lead to rejection and personal detail signals effort (measured, vendor surveys, snippet,
  https://topresume.com/career-advice/ai-in-hiring-survey). The figures don't compare; the direction is consistent.
- **What recruiters flag:** stock openers ("I am excited to apply", "I am writing to express my interest"),
  "Furthermore" and "Moreover" opening paragraphs, the same sentence skeleton in each paragraph, buzzwords
  ("expert", "cutting-edge"), and claims with no number, project or company named (practitioner, snippet,
  https://au.gradconnection.com/blog/employers/post/how-to-identify-ai-generated-graduate-resumes-and-cover-letters/).
- **Fix:** one specific example from the applicant's own work with its result, one reason this company and not
  another, in the applicant's own register. Every claim comes from the applicant and is true; never invent an
  achievement, a figure or a reason for applying.
- **Test:** could the letter go to another employer unchanged? Then it isn't finished.

## LinkedIn

A detector vendor flagged over half of long LinkedIn posts as likely AI and found average post length roughly
doubled after ChatGPT (measured, vendor detector, https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/28/linkedin_ai_posts). The
shapes readers named as annoying: performative charity, and a personal success turned into a general life lesson.
Model posts do the second readily (judgement). Tell what happened and stop; let the reader draw the lesson. No engagement
data was found, so the length point is a direction only.

## Slack and work chat

From workplace etiquette guides (practitioner, snippet, https://leaddev.com/communication/the-leaddev-guide-to-slack-etiquette):

- **Put the question in the first message.** "Hi, is the staging deploy blocked on the migration?", never a bare
  "Hi" and a wait.
- **One message,** not a run of short ones; reply in the thread; @channel only when it matters to everyone there.
- **One or two plain lines.** A model tends to write Slack like a memo: greeting, headings, bullets, bold, sign-off
  (judgement). Match how the channel already talks.

## Where the evidence is thin

The email-length data is old (2016) or a vendor's; the pronoun study and the cover-letter flags were read only
through summaries; the Coursera page on AI cover letters held no usable list and was dropped. The support and
apology rules are the best-supported part of this file.
