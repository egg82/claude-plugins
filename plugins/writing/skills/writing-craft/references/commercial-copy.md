# Commercial copy

Product descriptions, ads, email subject lines, brand social posts and anything else written to sell. Landing pages
and the general web-copy method (decide the page's job, use the customers' words, name the result) are in
`conversational-and-web-copy.md`; this file adds what is specific to selling a product.

## The hard rule: never write a review or testimonial in a customer's voice

The FTC's rule on fake reviews (16 CFR Part 465, final August 2024) prohibits fake or false reviews and
testimonials, AI-generated ones included, that misrepresent who wrote them or what they experienced. It also bars
buying or spreading them when the business knew or should have known, paying for a particular sentiment, undisclosed
reviews by employees, managers or relatives, company-run sites posing as independent review sites, and suppressing
negative reviews by threats. Knowing violations carry civil penalties (policy,
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials).

So: never draft a customer review, testimonial, star rating or quote with a plausible name and a first-person
experience, whatever the request calls it (sample, placeholder, illustration). Use real customers' words, attributed
and unaltered in meaning. A placeholder testimonial that ships is a fake review.

This also follows from the skill's rule against fabricating first-hand stories, and it holds outside the US as a
matter of honesty even where the FTC doesn't reach.

## Product descriptions: facts first

- **Missing facts lose the sale.** State materials or ingredients, every dimension with its unit, exact compatible
  model numbers and what is in the box. In Baymard's testing, every desktop shopper who needed ingredients dismissed
  a product that lacked them, and shoppers who couldn't find a detail spent one to three minutes hunting or left
  (measured, https://baymard.com/research-articles/product-descriptions).
- **Pair a dimension with a plain comparison:** "seat height 46 cm / 18 in", and "fits in the palm of your hand";
  never "standard height" (Baymard, above).
- **State what it doesn't contain** where shoppers ask ("BPA-free").
- **Structure a complex product as feature highlights:** a short headline, one paragraph on why it matters, an image
  or icon, for each of the product's main features; secondary features under their own heading. Shoppers found
  plain text blocks and bullet lists bland and read less of them. Simple accessories need only text and bullets
  (measured, usability testing, https://baymard.com/research-articles/structure-descriptions-by-highlights).
- **The model tell is mood in place of a fact:** "premium craftsmanship", "timeless design", "elevate your space",
  where a material, a measurement or a model number belonged. Every product in a batch gets the same template of
  equal-weight bullets. Read a model's descriptions side by side (`llm-tells.md`, across a batch).
- **People prefer human-written product descriptions** when they can't see the source, while LLM judges prefer LLM
  copy (measured, preliminary, small sample, snippet, https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12856). A model grader can't be the
  verdict on commercial copy.

## Voice of the customer

Collect answers from surveys, interviews, usability tests and public reviews, tag the phrases customers repeat, and
put their wording in the copy (practitioner, Joanna Wiebe, snippet,
https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/joanna-wiebe-conversion-copywriting). Copy that could describe any product in
the category answers no objection and uses no customer's phrase; this is the fix. It also keeps the hard rule above:
customers' words go in as claims you write, or as attributed quotes, never as invented reviews.

## Ads and headlines

**The headline carries the ad;** promise a specific benefit there. Ogilvy's claim that five times as many people
read the headline as the body is 1980s print with no test cited, and tests since have found long copy often beats
short when the product needs explaining (practitioner, weak, secondary,
https://swipefile.com/five-times-as-many-people-read-the-headlines-david-ogilvy). Take from it that the headline
must be specific; the ratio itself is unsupported.

## Subject lines

No usable measured rule was found. The one figure is a 2012 report favouring very short lines, and vendor claims of
an ideal length cite no source (opinion, https://experienceleaguecommunities.adobe.com/adobe-marketo-engage-27/best-practice-for-the-length-of-email-subject-lines-194453).
Over half of opens are on phones, which show about 25 characters, so put the specific part first. Test on the
actual list rather than trusting a length rule.

## Brand social

No evidence specific to brand posts was gathered. Apply the chat and post tells in
`conversational-and-web-copy.md` (emoji as structure, even agreeable affect, uniform polish) and the batch check in
`llm-tells.md`, since a brand account is read as a run of posts.

## Can readers tell?

- **Readers who handle many samples usually can,** across languages and domains, and concreteness and variety are
  where model text falls shortest (measured, Wang et al., in `other-languages.md`). When they couldn't tell the
  source, they didn't always prefer the human text (same study). Write concrete and varied.
- **Reviews are the exception, and the lesson there is about polish.** People scored about chance at telling real
  product reviews from LLM-written ones and caught only about a third of the fakes; they suspected flawless,
  positive, polished writing and were often wrong. LLM judges called nearly everything real (measured,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13313). Readers won't catch a fake review.

## Where the evidence is thin

Product descriptions are well supported by Baymard's testing. Ads, subject lines and brand social rest on weak or
no evidence here; Mailchimp, Litmus and Return Path's reports are where measured subject-line data would be.
