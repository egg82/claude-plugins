# Speech and scripts

Text written to be heard: speeches, presentations, broadcast and podcast scripts, video voice-over and audio drama.
A listener cannot reread, slow down or look back, so every rule here serves one listener hearing it once.

**Several of the skill's tells invert for the ear.** Repetition, triads and signposts are faults on the page and
working tools aloud; the fault is using them everywhere. The table says where.

| On the page | Aloud |
|---|---|
| Lists of three are a tell (`llm-tells.md`) | Rhythmic triads work "provided the device is not overused" (practitioner, CRS, below) |
| Repeated structure across a batch is a tell | Repetition with variation, anaphora and antimetabole make lines memorable ("I have a dream") (practitioner, snippet, Lehrman's *Political Speechwriter's Companion*, https://prorhetoric.com/new-book-for-political-speechwriters) |
| Signposting ("In this article we will") is a tell (`explanations-and-essays.md`) | Aural signposts ("second", "finally") help a listener keep place (CRS) |
| Em dashes and semicolons are punctuation choices | Punctuate for the ear; colons and semicolons build compound sentences a listener struggles to follow (CRS) |

A model tends to stack these devices in every paragraph at an even cadence (judgement); a speechwriter places one
where the speech turns. Count them in the draft and keep the ones doing work.

## Sentences

- **Write the way you speak, to one listener.** Contractions; everyday words ("but", not "however"; "robbery", not
  "heist"); subject first, active voice (practitioner, Kern's *Sound Reporting* via https://jea.org/?p=1953).
- **Spoken sentences average 8 to 16 words,** against up to 30 on the page (practitioner, Congressional Research
  Service, *Speechwriting in Perspective*, https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/98-170.html).
- **One thought per sentence, one breath per sentence.** An "and", "but" or "or" often marks two thoughts; split
  them (practitioner, UBC workshop, https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:LFS400/Workshops/Writing_For_the_Ear).
- **Avoid what trips the mouth or the ear:** homonyms, acronyms, tongue twisters, clause-heavy sentences that leave
  the speaker short of breath or put the stress in the wrong place (UBC; practitioner, secondary summary of Peggy
  Noonan's *On Speaking Well*, https://www.shortform.com/summary/on-speaking-well-summary-peggy-noonan).
- **One telling detail,** never a pile of adjectives (UBC).

## Numbers and names

- **Round figures** ("nearly 15,000") and **comparisons for measurements** ("the size of a football field") (UBC).
- **Limit names and numbers per sentence;** a listener holds few (Kern, above). Statistics read aloud in strings
  are what broadcast guides cut.

## Shape

- **No more than three major points** in a speech (CRS).
- **Broadcast news skips rhetorical questions** ("Have you ever wondered...?"), since the listener can't answer
  (Kern). Speeches use them more freely; models often open with one (judgement), so cut the opener and keep
  any that the speech answers in its own words.
- **Avoid stock openers** like "I'm happy to be here" (opinion, vendor skill page, snippet,
  https://www.agensi.io/skills/speech-draft).
- **Echo endings are a gimmick; a twist lands better in the last third than at the very end** (UBC). A model tends to
  end on a callback to its opening or a closing abstraction about why it all matters (judgement). End on the last real point.
- **Length:** broadcast copy runs about three words a second (UBC); a speech about 130 words a minute (opinion,
  vendor page, above). Noonan's limit for a speech is about 20 minutes.
- **Humour matters** in a speech (Noonan, above), and it is the one place listeners told AI and human readings apart
  (below). `humour.md` covers making it land.

## Check it aloud

- **The mouth edit:** say the opening from memory, compare with the page, and fix whatever was awkward to say
  (UBC). Read the whole draft aloud and mark what is hard to say (Noonan).
- **Silent reading cannot judge a script.** Have it read aloud by the person who will deliver it, or by a
  text-to-speech voice as a cheap first pass, and listen.

## Audio drama format

Sound cues get their own capitalised, numbered lines (SFX); scene headings give place and time ("INT. COFFEE SHOP
- DAY"); setting is built from sound, since there is nothing to see; a page of dialogue runs about a minute
(practitioner, snippet, https://theformatpage.yolasite.com/sonic-format.php). Characters explaining what the
listener can't see is the exposition trap here; no source on handling it was found.

## What listeners can tell

No study of listeners detecting LLM-written scripts was found. The one listener study tested the voice: with the
same script read by a person and by a synthetic voice, listeners called each human at about the same rate, and only
humour separated them, with fewer finding the AI read funny. Being told the read was AI made a fifth of listeners
like it less, against 4% for a human read (measured, trade-press survey, n=1,326, two short ad scripts,
https://radioink.com/2026/07/07/radio-listeners-cant-detect-ai-voice-but-dont-trust-it-either/). The script's
tells are therefore the page tells heard aloud; judge with listeners who use LLMs heavily (`judging.md`).

## Where the evidence is thin

BBC and NPR writing guides, and Noonan's and Lehrman's own text, were not reached; the speechwriter rules come from
summaries and a government report. The page-to-ear table is practitioner consensus, not measured.
